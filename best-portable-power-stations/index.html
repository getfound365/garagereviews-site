How to choose a portable power station

Capacity vs runtime. Capacity in watt-hours (Wh) sets how long a station runs. For phones, lights, and a laptop through a short outage, a 288–300Wh station is enough. For a fridge for a few hours, look at the 1kWh-class stations here — 960Wh to 1,070Wh. For a full day of outage essentials, the 2,042Wh Jackery Explorer 2000 v2 is the biggest station in this roundup. A bigger battery only helps if you can recharge it — from a wall outlet, a car, or solar panels — so check the recharge time on the station's review page before sizing up.

Output watts vs what it can run. Output in watts (W) sets what a station can power at once. A 300W station covers phones, laptops, lights, and a CPAP; a 600W station adds small appliances; a 1,500W–2,000W station can run a fridge, a coffee maker, or a small microwave one at a time. Yes — a 1kWh-class station with at least 1,500W of output can run a fridge for a few hours, one load at a time. The Pecron F1000LFP ran a refrigerator for six hours during a scheduled outage, according to one buyer on its review page. The DELTA 3 Classical 1000's review page states it cannot run 240V loads, central air, or a well pump — these are 120V battery stations, not whole-home backup.

Weight and portability. Small stations are genuinely portable — the Jackery Explorer 300 LiFePO4 is 7.5 lb and the EcoFlow RIVER 3 Plus is a 10.4 lb carry — while the 1kWh-class stations run 23.8–26.7 lb and the 2,042Wh Jackery Explorer 2000 v2 is a 39.5 lb carry. If the station moves between the house, the car, and the campsite, weight matters as much as capacity; if it sits by the fridge during outages, it doesn't. All eleven stations here are battery stations, not gas generators: no fuel, no fumes, no noise, and safe to run indoors.
