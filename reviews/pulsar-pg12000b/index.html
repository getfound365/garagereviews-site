The verdict

On watts per dollar, nothing in today’s set touches it: 12,000 peak / 9,500 running watts for $899, down from $1,299, dual fuel, electric start, and a 14-50R outlet that feeds a transfer switch properly. If the job is whole-home backup — well pump, fridge, freezer, lights, and a furnace fan at once — this is the only unit in today’s batch sized for it, and the price is $400 off.

The honest catch is the rating, and it is not a small one: 3.9 stars from 169 ratings, the weakest in the set, and the reviews behind it are a split, not a consensus. William (5 stars, Oct 14, 2024) ran it “24/7 non stop” for two days through Hurricane Helene and then five days through Milton, getting 14 to 16 hours from a 30-pound propane tank: “could not ask for a better generator.” Em (1 star, Oct 2, 2024) reports the opposite: “Burnt up after being used a couple of times” after a tornado, and “DO NOT RECOMMEND.” Both are real buyers of the same unit. Add the two spec-sheet flags — no CO auto-shutoff, and a CARB conflict the listing does not resolve — and this is a buy with your eyes open, not a default pick. If the rating worries you, the Champion 6500W at $999 costs $100 more, makes half the watts, and carries a 4.9-star record with CO Shield.

Who it’s for

Homeowners who need whole-home watts and are comfortable with an open-frame generator: 74 dB, 209 lb, and a machine you wheel out, fuel up, and run hard. The 14-50R outlet and 120/240V output make it transfer-switch ready, and the 8-gallon tank gives 12 hours at 50% load. It is not for campgrounds or close neighbors — 74 dB is loud — and it is not for anyone who wants a CO sensor, because this unit does not have one. It is also not a California buy on the listing’s word alone: Pulsar’s own sheet says CARB Certified: No.

Performance in real use

The buyer evidence is the split described above, and it is worth reading in full because the two poles are both storm stories. The 5-star pole is William’s five-day hurricane run on propane, 14–16 hours per 30-pound tank, running non-stop. The 1-star pole is Em’s unit burning up after a couple of uses following a tornado. With 169 ratings behind a 3.9 average, the truth is probably that most units run fine and a real minority fail early — which is exactly the pattern a 3.9-star average describes. What the record does not show is a buyer complaining about the wattage: nobody says it failed to carry the load. The practical read is to buy it, break it in, load-test it before the storm, and keep the receipt and the warranty — Pulsar covers it for 3 years limited — rather than discovering a problem at 2 a.m. in an outage.

Setup

Electric start with a recoil backup; the battery is included, along with a propane hose, spark plug wrench, and mobility kit. The 8-gallon tank takes gasoline, or hook up a propane tank — the unit switches fuels without a conversion kit. The 14-50R outlet is the transfer-switch connection; the four 5-20R household outlets cover extension cords. This unit has no CO auto-shutoff, so the safety rules are on you, not the machine: outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the house, exhaust pointed away from windows and doors, never in a garage even with the door open, and a battery CO alarm inside the house whenever it runs. Check your local rules on CARB before buying — Pulsar’s own sheet says CARB Certified: No.
