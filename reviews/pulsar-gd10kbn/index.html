The verdict

The GD10KBN is the inverter answer to the whole-home question at $779: 8,000 running watts of dual-fuel power with clean inverter output for sensitive electronics, a 14-50R RV outlet, and electric start — $220 off list inside Black Friday week, and the only wattage variant on the listing that is actually in stock. For a Thanksgiving-week outage buy, that combination of watts, dual fuel, and inverter power is the strongest value in today’s batch.

The honest catches are two, and both are on this page on purpose. First, the noise: do not believe the 50 dB line in the listing copy. The spec block says 68 dB and a buyer who measured it says roughly 72–75 dB — this is open-frame noise, not whisper-quiet inverter noise, and you should plan the placement accordingly. Second, the rating: the 602-rating headline covers every variant on the listing, so the 10,500W unit’s own record is the thinner 3.97 from 37. Buy it for the watts and the price, not for the star count.

Who it’s for

Anyone who needs whole-home backup — fridge, lights, furnace fan, well pump, and electronics at once — without stepping up to a 12,000W open-frame unit. Dual fuel suits anyone who doesn’t want to depend on a single fuel, and the electric start and wheel kit suit anyone moving a 125 lb unit alone. It is not for anyone who needs genuinely quiet operation near neighbors or a bedroom window: at 68 dB or more, this one is heard. It is also not for anyone who wants a CO shutoff — none is listed for this model, so the outdoor-only rule and a battery CO detector indoors are non-negotiable.

Performance in real use

10,500 peak / 8,000 running watts on gas (9,400 peak / 7,200 running on propane) covers the whole-home job, and the inverter output keeps laptops and TVs safe. The 8-gallon tank runs up to 10 hours at half load. Buyer evidence is mixed in exactly the way the rating split suggests: H, writing in November 2026, said it “runs what my older generator could not,” and Lance found it quieter than his old generator and easy to set up — but Mark, after a first break-in run, wrote “it is a bit loud, but I plan on building a small shed for it,” and james was blunter: “as much noisier than fifty one decibels, it’s more like seventy two or five.” One buyer also reported missing washers for the wheel kit on arrival, and normal break-in metal debris on the magnetic oil plug. The pattern is consistent: the power is real, the noise claim is not.

Setup

Fill the 8-gallon tank with gas or hook up a propane tank, check the oil, and push the electric start button — the wheel kit goes on first, and check the hardware bag: one buyer found washers missing for the wheels. Break it in before the storm: run a first phase, then change the oil (the magnetic drain plug catches break-in debris, which is normal). Then the hard rule: at least 20 feet from the house, exhaust pointing away, never in a garage or under a porch roof. No CO shutoff is listed for this model — run it outdoors and keep a battery CO detector inside the house.

How it compares

Against the Pulsar G12KBN at $749, the GD10KBN is the inverter version of the same 457cc/8-gallon family: 1,500 fewer peak watts (10,500 vs 12,000) and 1,500 fewer running watts (8,000 vs 9,500), but quieter on paper (68 dB vs 74 dB), far lighter (125 lb vs roughly 210 lb), and — the part that matters today — in stock while the G12KBN is out of stock. Against the Westinghouse WGen9500DF at $999, it costs $220 less and gives up 1,500 running watts, but it is an inverter, so its power is clean enough for sensitive electronics.
